# Speaker notes: When Math Journals Pause Submissions

Jun Yin (UCLA). The slides carry very few words; the argument is in these notes, slide by slide.

## 1. When Math Journals Pause Submissions

Open with the scene: you finally prove a theorem, walk it over to the journal, and the journal says it is no longer taking papers. Ask the room why a journal would ever do that.

## 2. Emergency Editors’ Meeting

*On screen: Many opinions. No answers.*

The editors meet. Everyone has a suggestion, nobody has a good one. Let the audience guess what problem they are facing before the next slide reveals it.

## 3. What Should a Journal Be in the AI Era?

*On screen: Stop patching the old model. Design the new one.*

Every fix on the table so far is a patch on a model built for a world where writing a paper was slow. The better question is what we would build if we started today, with AI as a given.

## 4. A Framework: Four Eras of AI

The idea comes from reading Asimov’s robot stories years ago, where the future is told in stages. Pre-AI: only people. Near-AI, where we are now: people pose the problems, steer the direction and digest the proofs. Mid-AI: a small nudge from a person is enough. Far-AI: no people left, only machines. The rest of the talk asks what happens to mathematics and its journals in each era.

## 5. Does the Future Still Need Pure Math?

*On screen: Yes. You can’t wait until the day you need it.*

Even in the Far-AI era a small group of machines will still be assigned to pure mathematics. You cannot start the research on the day the application shows up: nobody invents Itô’s formula because they suddenly need to price a bond. Pure mathematics gives every future question from physics, statistics and engineering a higher starting point. That holds with or without humans.

## 6. A Higher Starting Point

*On screen: Pure math lets tomorrow’s problems start halfway up.*

The value of pure mathematics is that when physics, statistics or engineering comes with a new question, the work does not start from the ground. Whoever asks, human or machine, begins on top of everything already proved.

## 7. A Small Corner for Pure Math

*On screen: Even with no humans left, a few machines keep proving theorems.*

In the Far-AI world most machines will be busy with whatever the world needs that day. A small group will still be set aside for pure mathematics, for the same reason we fund it now: its results are needed long before anyone knows what for.

## 8. How Will Mathematics Be Stored?

*On screen: Nobody, not even a robot, can read everything.*

If mathematics is still needed, the next question is how it lives inside the body of knowledge. A machine that needs an old result cannot read the whole literature every time. It needs a structure. So what is the structure?

## 9. Before AI: Two Ways to Organize Papers

First a review of the Pre-AI era. Papers were organised in two ways. One is a tree pulled together by journals: to follow probability you read the Annals of Probability, and under it sit many papers. The other is a web: papers cite each other, so every citation is an arrow from one paper to another.

## 10. How an LLM Captures a Language

Before guessing the future, recall how today’s large language models capture a whole language. First the text is cut into tokens. Each token becomes a vector. Then attention decides how strongly every token looks at every other token. What the model has learned about the language sits in the weights that produce these vectors and attention patterns. This is the picture to borrow for mathematics.

## 11. Papers Become Tokens

*On screen: Borrowing the word: a math token can be a whole idea.*

In the far future mathematical knowledge will be tokenised. “Token” is borrowed from language models as an analogy: there a token is a short piece of a word, here it can be long, a whole idea. Mathematics will not live as complete articles. What is kept is the essential part: the main concepts, which method solves which problem, which trick works in which situation.

## 12. A Universe of Tokens

*On screen: Similar ideas point in similar directions.*

The tokens live in a vector space, like the token embeddings of today’s large language models. How close two tokens are is decided by their inner product, that is, by their directions. The space is full of meaningful directions: one direction is condition A, another is condition B, another is assumption X, and so on for fields, applications, results, methods and points of view. A token that leans toward a direction has that property.

## 13. Math Lives in the Network

*On screen: Not a pile of papers. Layers and layers of connections.*

So the mathematics of the future is not a collection of separate papers. It lives inside a network with a huge number of attention layers. A result is no longer a file on a shelf. It is part of the structure and the links inside that network.

## 14. What Makes Math Work Important?

*On screen: One way: it adds a new direction.*

There is more than one way for work to matter. The first is to add a new direction, a new angle, to the space. In probability, next to martingales and Markov chains, Itô contributed stochastic calculus: a direction that was not there before, and everything after can lean on it.

## 15. What Makes Math Work Important?

*On screen: Another way: it rewires the network.*

The importance of a piece of mathematical work shows in how much it changes the structure and the links inside the network. Take the work of Green and Tao, who showed that the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. They built this link, and its lasting effect is that the primes became tightly joined to Szemerédi’s theorem and the tools of additive combinatorics and ergodic theory. Work matters by its place in the whole system, more than by the single statement it proves. One paper alone may not count for much; a group of papers can change the structure.

## 16. Both Moves Work at Every Scale

Both kinds of contribution, a new direction and a new link, come in every size. Large: joining two fields, or building a big new field. Small: joining two small directions inside one field, or adding one small new concept. The measure is the same at every scale: how the network changes.

## 17. The Network in Each Era

Put the network on the four-era timeline. Pre-AI: no network yet, only the journal tree and the citation web from before. Near-AI, now: the network is starting to be built, by people and machines together. Mid-AI: it has just been finished. Far-AI: it is long built and runs by itself. With this in place we can ask what a mathematics journal is in each era.

## 18. The Journal in the Mid-AI Era

*On screen: Small robots prove. One big robot sums up and updates the network.*

In the Mid-AI era a crowd of small robots does the work. One big robot collects what they found, sorts it, sums it up and updates the network. The journal is no longer a stack of separate papers. It becomes something like “The Math Network, This Week”: a regular report on how the network changed. People still read it.

## 19. The Journal in the Near-AI Era

*On screen: Still people submitting to people. The question is what the work changes.*

In the Near-AI era the editor is still a person and the author is still a person, now working together with a machine. After writing many papers, the author sums them up in one piece that explains how this body of work changes the network, and submits that. People still submit and people still referee. The focus moves from what one piece of work proves to what the work changes.

## 20. Near-AI, Pattern 1: Ten Papers, One Story

*On screen: Lean and AI check the proofs. The article is about the impact.*

One common pattern in the Near-AI era. A group works on a problem for a while and puts ten papers online. Lean and various AI tools verify them. Then the group writes one article that sums up the research: what was done, what the new ideas are. The subject of the article moves from the proofs to the mathematical impact. How credit is given is a separate question, left for later.

## 21. Near-AI, Pattern 2: The Connector

*On screen: The same idea and the same tool, spotted across fields, old and new.*

A second pattern, more unusual by today’s standards. Someone gathers work from different directions and different fields, old and new, and shows that the same ideas and the same tools are at work in all of them. The contribution is the connection itself. Again, how to give credit is a separate question.

## 22. Other Patterns?

*On screen: Guesses. We don’t know yet.*

These three are guesses, hence the question mark. When proving gets cheap, what surrounds the proof gets valuable. One: an article that proves nothing and only poses a good problem or points to a direction worth working on. Two: Lean and AI have verified a result but nobody understands why it holds, so the contribution is an explanation people can follow. Three: a record of what does not work, counterexamples and failed approaches, so that neither people nor machines try them again.

## 23. Times Are Changing

*On screen: The department’s tenure wall, a few years from now.*

A joke to open the topic. Look at the tenure wall. The first photo: a colleague of mine, possibly the last professor in the hundred-year history of this university to earn tenure with proofs made entirely by hand. Everyone after him has been playing the LLM lottery. A few photos later, the fifth one: the first professor tenured on AI work alone. The times are changing, and they are changing for the people in this room.

## 24. What We Have Already Started

*On screen: Small changes, already under way.*

Briefly, the changes we have already started making. Learning to turn papers into Lean so that proofs can be machine-checked. Writing skills: reusable written instructions that tell the AI how we want a kind of work done. Doing research with a multi-agent structure, for example a roadmap group, an exploration group and an analysis group.

## 25. The First Generation of Math Students in the AI Era

You are the first generation of mathematics students in the AI era, and the worries are real. One: you play the LLM lottery, never hit a big problem, and end up with no results. Two: you hit the jackpot, and people say the result belongs to the AI, not to you. Three: you study properly and stay away from the lottery, and you cannot produce results that stand out.

## 26. A Way In: Fill One Small Patch

*On screen: Pick a small region. Use AI to fill it in. That region is your work.*

One way to begin. Choose a small region where a few results are known but the picture is thin, like a thinly charted stretch of an old map: someone has been there, but only a handful of results exist. Use AI to work that region deeply until it is filled in. Then the claim you make is about the region: I completed the work on this patch. No jackpot is needed, and what is yours is the choice of the patch and the fact that it is now complete. The test is whether the student can say plainly what happened there: in this small patch, with these tools and these ideas, these problems were settled.

## 27. Then Grow It

*On screen: The same moves, one size up each time.*

And then? Grow it. Fill a second patch next to the first, and join the two: that link is itself a contribution, the second kind from earlier. Keep going and the scale goes up, from a patch to a region. It is the picture from the slide on scale, read as a career: start small, and repeat the same two moves one size up each time. Where to expand next is the part the machine cannot choose for you.

## 28. Then Look Further

*On screen: Far away, find what is useful to your region, and connect it.*

Keep expanding, in a second way. Take the magnifying glass and search further out, in parts of the map far from your own region. There you find things that are useful for, or related to, what you already have, and you draw the line that joins them to your region.

## 29. From My Trees to My Garden

*On screen: A student’s work used to be a few points. Now it is a region.*

For a student the difference is this. Before AI you grew a tree, or two, and you said: that is my work, two or three results. Now you tend a garden, a small region, and you say: that is my garden. Start small, let it grow, look further out for what belongs with it. If you can make a small, good-looking garden and say clearly what is in it, that is very good work.

## 30. No More Overnight Fame

*On screen: Names will be made by twenty years in one region, not by one theorem.*

First prediction: no more overnight fame. Before AI, someone could solve one famous problem and be known the next morning. Think of Yitang Zhang in 2013: a little-known lecturer proves bounded gaps between primes, and everyone agrees he is extraordinary. In the Near-AI era the same event will be read differently. People will say it was a lucky pull, or that it cost a few million dollars of tokens. But look at the picture on the right: fame does not disappear, it moves. A name will be made the slow way, by ten or twenty years in one field. You stand on a staircase built from your own work, a whole region of results, and people say: she built that. It may be a smaller fame than proving Fermat’s Last Theorem once brought, but it is one you can earn without winning a lottery.

## 31. The Referee Gets a Checklist

*On screen: Machines check the proof. The referee checks whether it matters.*

Second prediction: refereeing turns into a short form. The first two boxes are ticked before the referee even opens the file: Lean has checked the proof, and an AI has checked it again. What is left for the human is below the line. How important is the work: a new direction, a new link, or only a new PDF? And is the topic interesting or boring? The slow part of refereeing, checking correctness, goes to the machines. The part that stays with people is judgement.

## 32. The New Erdős Number

*On screen: Almost everyone has ChatGPT number 1.*

Third prediction, about corridor gossip. The Erdős number counts how many coauthor steps separate you from Paul Erdős. Its replacement is the ChatGPT number. Almost everyone has number 1, because almost everyone has written a paper with it. So the gossip changes: A whispers to B that C has ChatGPT number 2. C has never worked with the machine directly, only with a coauthor who did. C is at the board, working by hand, and has not noticed.

## 33. Five Years of Management Experience

*On screen: Every math student will have run a team. Of agents.*

Fourth prediction. A math major goes to a job interview at a company. The interviewer asks: how many years of management experience? Five, says the student. The interviewer is startled, because this is a fresh graduate. What the student has in mind is graduate school: five years of running a dozen small agents that wrote the papers. Setting the direction, handing out the lemmas, checking what came back. So here is a cheerful side effect for you: doing research with AI is also practice in running a team, and that is a skill the rest of the world pays for.

## 34. Don’t Gamble Your Last Coffee Money

*On screen: The LLM lottery pays in theorems. Mathematicians still run on coffee.*

And one warning, for the professors in the room. You know the old advice: never gamble with the money for the baby’s milk. Here is our version: never spend your last coffee money on the LLM lottery. A mathematician, as the saying goes, is a machine for turning coffee into theorems. The lottery may pay out one big theorem, or nothing at all. The coffee always works.

## 35. Declaration of Human Use

*On screen: Today papers declare their use of AI. Soon it goes the other way.*

One last joke. Many journals now ask for a declaration of AI use. The authors write that the AI only polished the English, that they initiated the project, and that they take full responsibility. Now read the footnotes: the AI also proved Lemmas 2 to 9, and it joined the project later, about five minutes later. Give it a few years and the form flips: the AI writes a declaration of human use. A human was used to apply for the grant. A human helped to choose the notation. And the acknowledgement: we thank the human for paying the subscription and electricity bills on time. Notice that it does not say who chose the problem. I like to think that was still us.

## 36. Submissions are open again.

*On screen: To submit your manuscript, please check the box below.*

We started with a journal that paused submissions. Here is how the story ends, in the Far-AI era, when there are no people left, only machines. The journal is open again. To submit your manuscript, you only have to tick one box. Stop here and let the room read the box.
